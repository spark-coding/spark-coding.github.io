A parent's guide

Your Child's Coding Journey

Coding is a long-term skill, like reading or music. This guide shows what the journey looks like at Spark Coding, from a child's first lines of code to the opportunities open to teenagers who keep at it.

At a glance

  • A clear, age-mapped path from Year 3 through Year 12.
  • Personalised learning, with a maximum of 3 students per class.
  • Optional doors along the way, like competitions, scholarships and real-project portfolios. None need to be decided up front.
  • Honest about timing: most children spend one to two years at each stage.

The stages of the journey

Four stages, each building on the last. Older beginners often skip the earlier stages entirely.

1

First Code

Ages 8–9 · Year 3 · Scratch

Learning to think in code by building animations and simple games. Children move from playing games to making them, a quietly transformative shift in how they see technology.

By the end of the stage: can build a complete game with score-keeping, multiple levels, and characters that respond to the player.

2

Bridge to Real Code

Ages 9–10 · Years 3–4 · MakeCode Arcade

Building arcade games with familiar blocks, then flipping to read the real Python code underneath. It’s the first taste of what professional programmers see on screen, with the blocks they already know as a safety net. Games can be loaded onto a handheld micro:bit console.

By the end of the stage: can recognise the same code in two forms, blocks and Python, and make small text edits with confidence.

3

Python

Ages 10–12 · Years 4–6 · Python

The same language used in real software and at university. Children write Python from scratch and build small, useful programs, like quizzes, calculators and word games, that they can show off to family and friends.

By the end of the stage: a portfolio of half a dozen working programs they wrote themselves, and the vocabulary to read code in any modern language.

4

Building Skills

Ages 12+ · Year 7 onwards · Python

Bigger programs and more sophisticated games, with real data, searching, sorting and testing. Students write bots that battle in Spark Arena tournaments. Those who want it can prepare for national programming competitions.

By the end of the stage: a substantial portfolio of real projects. Whether to take coding further is fully in the student’s hands.

A note for older beginners

If your child is starting in Year 5 or later, we don't rewind them through the earlier stages. A Year 7 beginner typically starts at Stage 3 (Python), building real programs from the very first lesson. The stages are a map, not a queue.

How we choose where to start

The 45-minute trial doubles as a placement check. We see what your child already knows, what excites them, and what project would feel meaningful to build. By the end of the trial, we'll have a clear, personalised plan for the next term.

Where this can lead

Three doors that open progressively. None are required. Your child can step through any, all, or none, and still come out a stronger thinker.

Door 1 · Every student

A stronger learner

Computational thinking is now part of the NSW curriculum. Students who code think more clearly, debug their own work, and break big problems into smaller ones. Those skills show up in maths, science, and even essay writing.

Door 2 · From around Year 3, opt-in

Friendly national competitions

Australia runs short online competitions every year that are fun, low-cost and designed to be enjoyable: Bebras (Feb) and AMC (Aug) from Year 3, CAT (May) and the free Raspberry Pi Foundation Coding Challenge (June) from Year 5, and from Year 7 the Australian Informatics Olympiad . We register and prepare students in class, with no extra work at home.

Door 3 · From Year 7, requires sustained effort

Scholarships, university, and beyond

For students who keep coding seriously into high school, sustained competition results are recognised by private-school scholarship panels, university bonus-point schemes, and international university applications. Just as importantly, the portfolio of real projects students build with us is a strong signal in its own right, from Year 9 work-experience applications through to university interviews.

How you'll know your child is on track

After one term

They'll show you a small program they built themselves. You'll start hearing them ask, "What if I made it do this instead?"

After one year

They'll fix their own bugs without needing to ask, and have made something they're genuinely proud of.

After three years

They'll read and write the same kind of code professional programmers use, have at least one competition certificate, and know whether coding is "a fun side thing" or something they want to keep doing.

What the journey can look like

Three illustrative examples (not real students). Each child starts at a different age and takes a different path.

Mia, starts in Year 3

First year: builds her first Scratch animations and sits Bebras for fun. Year 4: makes her first arcade-style game and sits the AMC. Year 5: moves into Python, sits her first CAT, and builds a recipe-recommendation app for the school showcase. By Year 7 she's writing real programs and sitting her first Australian Informatics Olympiad.

Eli, starts in Year 6

No coding experience, but wants a head start before high school. Spends his first term in Python building small, real programs like a quiz game and a Mad Libs generator. By the end of Year 7 he sits his first Australian Informatics Olympiad, and by Year 9 he's confident enough to help younger students.

Aanya, starts in Year 4

Does Scratch and the bridge-to-Python stage through Years 4 and 5, picks up Python in Year 6, and sits AMC and Bebras every year. When she sits a private-school scholarship at the end of Year 6, her competition results and portfolio of Python projects give her plenty to talk about. Whatever the outcome, she starts Year 7 confident and capable.
